Lesson 4

Collections

Many things, and what many costs.  A List holds things -- the rules are yours to write.

TimingTwo meetings (~50 min each).  Closes Unit 1.
New ideaA collection is itself an object, with its own state and its own rules
Builds onLessons 1 through 3.  Your dice are fixed once built, they arrive valid, and their behavior lives beside them.  The container is the last thing left to design.
StandardWrite a type that owns a collection and every rule about it -- what may go in, how many, who may change it, and what happens when it runs out.

Warm-up (~7 min)

Run it before you change anything.  Six scenes, and every one of them is a List doing exactly what a List does, in a place that needed something with rules.  Then one question, and it has a real answer:  DiceBag.Add() checks the capacity and refuses politely.  Scene 2 puts nine dice in a bag of six anyway.  Who is at fault?

Direct instruction (~13 min)

Run it first, then ask who is at fault

Six scenes of output on the projector, read against DiceBag.cs.  Give them a minute with it before you say anything.

Then:  "Add() checks the capacity and refuses.  Scene 2 puts nine dice in a bag of six.  Who is at fault?"

Take real answers.  Somebody will blame Add, somebody will blame List, and both did their jobs exactly.  The fault is that a rule was written where anybody could walk around it.

Put this on the board and leave it all lesson:  a rule with a bypass is a suggestion.

Scene 4 is the beat that matters

Draw it as boxes, the way Lesson 1 did.  init sealed the slot.  The List the slot points at is still wide open.

The sentence to say out loud:  making the reference fixed and making the thing it points at fixed are two separate decisions. Lesson 1 made the first one, and today makes the second.

This is also the honest answer to any student who felt Lesson 1 overclaimed.  It did, slightly, and this is where the rest of it arrives.

Leave the empty-bag question open, and count the room

Throw, null, or Try -- take a show of hands and write the counts on the board.

Revisit those numbers at the milestone, when somebody's deck runs out mid-game and the choice starts having consequences they can feel.

The answer worth steering toward is a question:  is running out normal for this caller, or is it a bug?  That is Lesson 2's distinction, arriving in a new place.

Guided activity (~22 min)

  1. @{h=Step 2 is the one to protect; p=System.Object[]}
  2. @{h=Let them find Step 7 early; p=System.Object[]}

Common mistakes to watch for

  • A shuffle that picks from the whole list every iteration.  It runs, it looks shuffled, and it is measurably biased.  Mention it; leave it alone unless somebody wants the histogram from Lesson 3 and an afternoon.
  • RemoveAt(0) rather than the end. Correct, and O(n) per draw.  Fine at 52 cards.  Say so, and say why you are bothering to say so.
  • Returning _contents from some other method after carefully hiding the field.  The hole reopens quietly.  Ask what the return type is.
  • The cast escape hatch. ((List<Dice>)bag.Contents).Clear() compiles and works, because the read-only view hands back the same object.  Somebody will find it.  It is real, and the fixes are AsReadOnly() or returning a copy.  The interesting question is whether the type is protecting against an accident or an attacker -- and it is nearly always an accident.
  • A Deck that shuffles by sorting on a random key. It works.  Ask what happens when two keys collide, then let it stand.

Wrap-up (~5 min)

Ask: You picked a way for Draw to report an empty bag.  Which one, and what would change if your game had fifty decks running at once?

Looking for: A defence tied to who is calling.  A programmer's bug wants a throw.  A game loop that expects to run out wants the Try pattern.  The student who says "it depends on whether running out is normal" has all of Lesson 2 in one sentence.

Teaching notes

  • This closes Unit 1. The milestone follows:  two meetings to build a playable card game out of all four lessons.  Say that out loud at the end of Meeting B, because it reframes the Cards work as the start of something rather than the end.
  • What Unit 2 needs from this lesson. Deck and Hand are the same shape carrying different rules.  That observation is what Lesson 7 cashes in when Player and Creature want a base class and get parts instead.  Plant it in the Meeting B wrap-up and leave it there.
  • Three Lesson 1 debts get paid here: { get; private set; }, deep immutability, and IReadOnlyList<T> were all cut from Lesson 1 for being a second idea.  A bag's contents genuinely change, so mutation through methods is finally needed -- and the need is felt several scenes before the syntax arrives.

Rubric reference (no points)

Standard

Getting StartedThe build is red, or the list is still public.  Next step: make the field private readonly List<Dice> _contents = []; and add public IReadOnlyList<Dice> Contents => _contents;.  Then re-run and read what stops compiling -- CS1061 in Program.cs is the lesson working, and CS1061 inside Standard() is Step 7 arriving early.
Got It WorkingThe list is private, Draw removes and handles empty, Shuffle changes the bag, DrawnCount is { get; private set; }, and Standard() builds itself through Add().
Made It Mine ⭐…and I built a Deck and a Hand from my own cards, dealt a five-card hand, and scored it with both Lesson 3 rule sets -- and I can name one rule my Deck has that my Hand would find absurd.
Went Beyond…and I went after something the lesson left open -- a shuffle you can swap out for a different strategy, a Deck that survives being handed to somebody trying to break it, the cast that defeats IReadOnlyList and what to do about it, or a measurement showing that the naïve shuffle really is biased.

⭐ Made It Mine is the goal for everyone. “Went Beyond” keeps its list open -- the examples are only starting points. Going somewhere the lesson left open is the whole idea.
