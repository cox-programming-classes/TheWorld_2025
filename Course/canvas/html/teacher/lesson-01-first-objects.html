Lesson 1

First Objects

Complexity, and managing mutability.  Find the bug hiding inside working code.

TimingTwo meetings (~50 min each)
New ideaImmutability is declared member by member -- and record governs something else
Builds onAny language you already know.  It does assume you have written objects somewhere before.
StandardDeclare immutability member by member on a record, repair the call sites that depended on mutation, and explain what that bought.

Warm-up (~7 min)

You have shipped this bug before, in another language.  Somewhere, you changed a thing in one place and watched it change somewhere across the room.  What language were you in, and what was the thing -- a list, a dictionary, an object?  And whose job was it to stop that from happening?

Direct instruction (~13 min)

The shape they were taught, and the shape we are building

Name it directly.  These students learned OO somewhere Java-shaped, and that is the frame they will read every line of C# through unless you move it.

Two columns on the board.  Then, and this matters, hold them as equals:  "Both of these are defensible.  They are different bets about what goes wrong in a big program.  The second one is where C# has been going for ten years, and it is the one you have yet to meet."

Scene 1 shows the right-hand column already paying out:  two separately built 2d6+3s are ==, and rolling dice leaves the dice alone.

  • an object HAS state you change  |  a value IS its data
  • setSides(4)  |  build a different one and use it
  • equal if it is the SAME object  |  equal if it SAYS the same thing
  • behavior lives on the object  |  behavior can live beside it (L3)

What a variable holds

Scene 3, drawn on the board as boxes.  Then:  "Scene 2 is the second picture.  That is all it ever was."

Then string, immediately, because it is the bridge:  a string is a reference type that BEHAVES like a value, because it is fixed at creation.  Every one of them knows this, and today is the first time anyone has told them why it matters.  That is the whole design move, previewed.

Board diagram
int a = 5; int b = a;        [a|5]  [b|5]        two boxes, two fives

var first  = new Dice(...);  [first |*]--> (dice)
var second = first;          [second|*]--> (dice)  two boxes, one die

Picking a primitive

Still Scene 1.  Most have met the float thing.  Few have met integer division producing a silently wrong number that sails through every test.

Flag it hard -- it is the single most likely source of a wrong-but-running number in everything they write this semester.

Close with:  "A die comes in whole numbers, so int is right here.  It is right sometimes, and 'the default' is a poor reason to pick anything."

The one keyword

Only now, with the failure on the board behind you, put up { get; set; } vs { get; init; }.

The sentence to say out loud:  "Every option here is a choice.  { get; set; } is the one you make by staying quiet."

Then send them to step 4 and let the compiler do the teaching.

The one misconception to preempt -- and only this one

"record means immutable." Correct them:  Scene 2 is a record misbehaving exactly like any mutable object.  A record gives you value equality.  Immutability is a separate decision you make per member.

Several students will arrive believing records are immutable.  Good.  That belief is the thing this lesson exists to break, which is why Dice ships as a record with public setters.

Guided activity (~22 min)

  1. Everyone runs the starter before touching anything.  Three scenes, all working.
  2. Steps 2 and 3 as a class:  find the guilty line in Scene 2, then match it to a picture in Scene 3.
  3. Step 4 individually:  change all three to init, build, and READ the errors before fixing.  Circulate -- the instinct to immediately undo the change is strong.
  4. Step 5 as a class if the room is struggling.  The fix is to construct a second die, and some students will feel they cheated because it is more typing than the line it replaced.  Say plainly that it is more typing and it is the entire idea.
  5. Steps 6 through 8 are Meeting B and should be protected.  Do step 8 as a whole class, out loud, before they read it -- ask "did the aliasing go away?" and wait until somebody works out that it stayed.
  6. Release them to Cards for the back half of Meeting B. Circulate; resist answering.

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Step 5 "fixed" by putting set back. Watch for it.  The instruction is explicit and they will still do it once.
  • Step 6 attempted with == where ReferenceEquals belongs, which prints True and looks like it worked.  Excellent mistake and worth catching publicly:  == is True because the VALUES match, and it would be True for two entirely separate dice.  It says only that the values match.  This is step 10 arriving early, from a student, which is the best way for it to arrive.
  • Somebody will ask whether a shortcut exists for building a modified copy.  One does -- it is called with -- and it waits for a later lesson by design.  Write that student's name down and tell them they have found a later lesson.
  • Somebody will change set to init and then try to make Random immutable, or wonder why it stays mutable.  Take it seriously:  a Random with fixed internal state hands back the same number forever.  State earns its keep, and unmanaged state is the enemy.  Say it in those words.
  • Students who finish the Dice half in ten minutes and then stall on Cards.  That is the lesson working.  The Dice half is supposed to be quick.

Wrap-up (~5 min)

Ask: Name one thing about a set of dice you made impossible to change, and one thing you left changeable -- and say who would disagree with you.

Looking for: Anything specific and defended.  The tell is the second half of the question -- a student who can name a reasonable person who would have built it differently has judgment; a student who answers "this is just correct" has a rule they are following.  Both are fine on day one, but note which is which.

Teaching notes

  • Pacing. Meeting A: 7 warm-up / 13 instruction / 22 guided (steps 1–5) / 5 wrap.  Meeting B: 5 recap / 20 guided (steps 6–10) / 20 Cards / 5 wrap.
  • If Meeting A runs long, cut the primitives section and fold it into the Meeting B recap.  Protect the warm-up, and protect step 8 -- those are the two things this lesson is actually for.
  • The thing to protect is step 8. Everything else here is syntax they would have picked up from a reference page.  The reframe -- the sharing stayed and you made it stop mattering -- is what makes the whole functional-leaning style read as a trade with something on both sides.  Students who miss it spend the semester thinking immutability is a rule they are being asked to follow.
  • Held back on purpose: struct, private set, deep immutability, records holding collections, the heap and the stack, boxing, and with.  Every one of those is a good lesson, and every one belongs to a later day.  private set and deep immutability land in Lesson 4, where a Deck's contents genuinely change and the need is felt first.
  • If they finish early: point them at GameData/GameMechanics/Dice.cs in the finished game -- the same record, grown up, with a Parse and a WeightedDice that overrides Roll to cheat.  Leave virtual alone.  Let them ask, and write down who asked; that is Lesson 5's warm-up.
  • Expect and welcome the face-up/face-down argument in Cards.  Two answers are defensible and the disagreement IS the lesson.  Leave it open.  If you need a thumb on the scale, ask "who is holding the card, and do they know what it is?" and then get out of the way.

Rubric reference (no points)

Standard
Declare immutability member by member on a record, repair the call sites that depended on mutation, and explain what that bought.
Getting StartedThe build is red, or the properties still say { get; set; }.  Next step: change all three to { get; init; } and read the two errors carefully -- they are pointing at the two places that were relying on reaching into an object somebody else was also holding.  Fix those two lines and you are through.
Got It WorkingAll three properties are init-only, both scenes are repaired with set left where it lies, and the program runs.
Made It Mine ⭐…and I built my own Card with a deliberate call about what is fixed and what can change -- and I can name somebody who would have decided differently and say what they would have wanted.
Went Beyond…and I went after something the lesson left open -- an enum for rank or suit, a Card that refuses to exist in a nonsense state, a second version of a member I was torn about, or a question about Random and mutable state I chased down on my own.

⭐ Made It Mine is the goal for everyone. “Went Beyond” keeps its list open -- the examples are only starting points. Going somewhere the lesson left open is the whole idea.
